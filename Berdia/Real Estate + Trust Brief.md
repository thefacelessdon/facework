# DARIUS — REAL ESTATE CAREER SUPPORT THROUGH THE FAMILY TRUST

---

## What This Is About

You're inheriting a collection of precious coins. You're also starting a career in real estate in Texas. This brief explains how those two things can work together — not by selling the coins and spending the cash, but by using them as a foundation that gives you leverage most new agents don't have.

This is not about locking you into anything. It's about laying out a structure that amplifies your effort without putting you or the family's other assets at risk.

---

## The Starting Point: What You Have

The coin collection is real, tangible value — hard assets that can be documented, insured, appraised, and used as collateral. Most people entering real estate have nothing like that behind them.

The default option is simple: get the coins appraised, sell them, and use the cash. That's straightforward, but once sold, the asset is gone and your leverage resets to zero.

**The alternative: treat the coins as balance-sheet strength rather than money to be spent.**

---

## Step 1: Get the Coins Appraised and Documented

Before anything else, the collection needs a professional appraisal. This establishes:

- **Fair market value** — what the coins are actually worth (this number matters for everything that follows)
- **Itemized inventory** — each coin documented with description, condition, and value
- **Insurance basis** — allows proper coverage while in storage or transit
- **Collateral documentation** — lenders and the trust need a third-party valuation

**Who does this:** A certified numismatic appraiser (look for ANA or ASA credentials). Cost is typically $150–$500 depending on collection size.

**Timeline:** This should happen before any other decisions are made. Without an appraisal, everything below is theoretical.

---

## Step 2: Place the Coins into the Family Trust

If the coins are placed into the Family Operating Trust:

- The trust becomes the legal owner and custodian
- The coins are insured, documented, and protected from personal creditors or divorce exposure
- The trust's overall balance sheet is strengthened (lenders care about collateral diversity, not just income)

**What you receive in exchange:** The trust commits to providing deal-specific capital support as you build your practice, structured through formal agreements with defined terms.

**What the trust receives:** A diversified asset that strengthens its collateral position, plus access to real estate deal flow and returns from successful transactions.

**This is a disciplined exchange, not a gift in either direction.**

---

## Step 3: Build Your Career as a Licensed Agent

In Texas, you'll be licensed as a sales agent and work under a sponsoring broker. You earn commissions through that broker. Nothing about this structure changes that.

### What the Trust Can and Cannot Do

**The trust cannot:**

- Receive your commissions (those are earned by you through your broker)
- Appear on real estate contracts as a party (unless it's actually buying property)
- Interfere with TREC licensing rules or broker-agent relationships

**The trust can:**

- Provide transactional capital support on a deal-by-deal basis
- Fund earnest money deposits, appraisal gaps, or short-term liquidity needs
- Advance funds directly to escrow (not to you personally) with a documented promissory note
- Get repaid at closing from deal proceeds, with a defined fee or interest

### How a Deal-Specific Advance Works

1. You identify a live transaction where capital support would help (earnest money, appraisal gap, closing cost bridge)
2. You submit a written request to the Trustee specifying: property address, amount needed, purpose, expected closing date, and repayment source
3. The Trustee reviews and approves (or declines) based on deal specifics
4. Funds are advanced directly to the escrow/title company — not to your personal account
5. A promissory note is signed documenting: amount, interest/fee, repayment terms, and what happens if the deal falls through
6. At closing, the trust is repaid first from proceeds. You keep your commission and momentum
7. If the deal falls through, the promissory note defines how repayment works (timeline, collateral, or write-off provisions)

**This is not ongoing debt. It's tactical support tied to specific, real transactions.**

### TREC Compliance — Must Be Reviewed by Attorney

Texas Real Estate Commission rules govern agent conduct, disclosure requirements, and financial relationships. Before any trust-backed transaction support:

- **An attorney licensed in Texas must review** whether trust advances to escrow on deals where you are the agent require disclosure to buyers, sellers, or the broker
- **Your sponsoring broker must be informed** of any financial relationship that could create the appearance of undisclosed principal interest
- **If the trust is purchasing property** (see Phase 2 below), you may need to disclose your relationship to the trust as a related party

**This is not a barrier — it's a checkbox. But it must be checked before the first deal, not after.**

## Phase 1: Commission-Era Support (Years 1–3)

During your first years as an agent, the trust's role is simple: give you a balance sheet so you can compete.

**What this solves:** Most new agents lose deals not because they can't find them, but because they can't move fast, can't bridge gaps, or can't compete with cash-backed buyers.

With trust backing:

- You can write stronger offers
- You can keep deals alive that others lose to timing or cash requirements
- You can work with clients who need creative solutions
- You can build volume faster without personal financial risk

**Limits during Phase 1:**

- Maximum single advance: defined in trust operating rules (suggest $25K–$50K depending on coin appraisal value)
- Maximum total outstanding advances: defined cap (suggest $75K–$100K)
- Each advance requires Trustee approval and documentation
- No advances for speculative purposes — only for live, contracted transactions

**Phase 1 Gate — To move to Phase 2, you must demonstrate:**

- [ ] At least 12 months of active licensure
- [ ] Minimum number of closed transactions (suggest 5–10)
- [ ] All trust advances repaid on time
- [ ] Clean compliance record with TREC and your broker
- [ ] Written proposal for your first principal transaction

## Phase 2: Buying and Operating Property (Years 2–5+)

Once you've established yourself as an agent and demonstrated deal judgment, the trust can support a second lane: buying properties directly.

### How This Works

- Each property is purchased through a **property-specific LLC (SPV)** owned by the trust
- The trust provides capital. You source, underwrite, and manage the deal
- Risk is isolated — if a deal goes bad, losses are contained to that one LLC
- Profits flow back to the trust and are split according to an agreed structure

### Deal Types

| Type | Description | Typical Capital | Risk Level |
|------|-------------|-----------------|------------|
| Distressed / REO | Foreclosures, bank-owned properties | $30K–$75K | Medium |
| Tax sale | Properties sold for delinquent taxes | $10K–$50K | Medium-High |
| Fix and flip | Buy, renovate, sell | $50K–$150K | Medium |
| Buy and hold | Rental income properties | $75K–$200K | Lower |

### Deal Approval Process

1. You identify a property and prepare a written underwriting memo: purchase price, estimated renovation cost, comparable sales, expected timeline, and projected return
2. Trustee reviews the memo against trust capital allocation rules
3. If approved: SPV is formed, capital is deployed, you manage the project
4. At sale or stabilization: trust is repaid first, profits split per agreement
5. SPV is dissolved after the deal is complete

### Profit Split (Suggested Starting Framework)

| Party | Share | Rationale |
|-------|-------|-----------|
| Trust | 60–70% | Provided all capital, bears financial risk |
| Darius | 30–40% | Sourced deal, managed execution, contributed expertise |

The split can shift over time as you build track record and eventually contribute your own capital alongside the trust's.

### What the Trust Gets

- Diversified returns beyond solar and restaurant income
- Real estate assets on its balance sheet
- Deal flow from someone with local market knowledge
- Documented track record for future lending relationships

### What You Get

- Ability to act as a principal (buyer), not just a broker
- Real estate investment experience without personal debt
- Equity participation in deals you source and manage
- A track record that compounds over time

## Risk Containment

### If a Deal Falls Through (Phase 1 Advance)

- Advance amount is documented in a promissory note
- Repayment terms are defined before the advance is made
- If earnest money is forfeited, the loss is limited to the advance amount
- Advances stop until the matter is resolved
- No impact on solar income, restaurant, or other family assets

### If a Property Deal Goes Bad (Phase 2)

- Loss is contained to the SPV — the property-specific LLC
- Trust's exposure is limited to the capital deployed for that deal
- Other trust assets (land, solar, restaurant, coins) are not affected
- SPV is wound down, remaining assets (the property itself) are sold to recover what's possible

### If Real Estate Doesn't Work Out

- Coins remain in the trust as a balance-sheet asset
- Trust continues operating with solar and restaurant income
- Your career path is yours — the trust doesn't own your license or your time
- No ongoing obligations beyond repaying any outstanding advances
