### Financial Records — THIS IS THE BIG ONE

The restaurant's financials are the foundation for borrowing capacity. Lenders will not lend against "we think it makes $500K." They lend against documented, verifiable numbers.

**What you need (in order of importance):**

- [ ] **Federal tax returns for the last 3 years** (business returns — Schedule C if sole proprietorship, or 1065/1120 if entity). This is the minimum a lender will accept.
- [ ] **Profit and loss statements (P&L) for the last 3 years** — monthly or quarterly. If these don't exist in clean form, this is the first thing to fix.
- [ ] **Balance sheet** — current. Shows assets, liabilities, and equity. If the restaurant has never had a formal balance sheet, the CPA will need to create one.
- [ ] **Bank statements for the last 12 months** — the restaurant's operating account(s). Every account.
- [ ] **Point-of-sale (POS) reports** — if the restaurant uses a POS system (Square, Toast, Clover, etc.), pull the annual sales summaries. These verify revenue independently of tax returns.
- [ ] **Payroll records** — how many employees? Total payroll cost? Are payroll taxes current?
- [ ] **Any outstanding debts** — loans, credit cards, vendor balances, equipment financing
- [ ] **Accounts receivable** — does anyone owe the restaurant money? (Catering accounts, government contracts, etc.)
- [ ] **Accounts payable** — does the restaurant owe anyone money?

### What "Clean Financials" Means

A lender reviewing the restaurant will want to see:

1. **Revenue is verifiable** — tax returns match bank deposits match POS reports
2. **Expenses are categorized** — food cost, labor, rent, utilities, insurance, other
3. **Owner compensation is separated** — how much is Stanley drawing? Is it consistent? Is it documented as salary, draws, or informal?
4. **EBITDA is calculable** — Revenue minus operating expenses minus owner compensation = EBITDA. This number determines borrowing capacity.

**If the restaurant has been run informally** (cash transactions not fully recorded, expenses mixed with personal spending, no formal bookkeeping), the CPA will need time to reconstruct clean financials. This is common for family-owned restaurants. It is not a problem — it is a task that needs to start now.

## SECTION 5: LIFE INSURANCE POLICY

### Policy Documents

- [ ] **The complete life insurance policy** — not the summary, the full policy document. If you can't find the original, the insurance company can provide a duplicate.
- [ ] **Most recent annual statement** — shows current death benefit, cash value (if applicable), premiums, and policy status
- [ ] **Premium payment records** — is the policy current? Are premiums being paid? By whom?

### Key Information to Extract

| Question | Why It Matters |
|----------|---------------|
| **Policy type:** Term, whole life, universal life, or variable? | Determines whether there's cash value and how the policy can be used |
| **Death benefit amount:** $_____ | Determines how much flows to beneficiaries or the trust at death |
| **Cash value (if whole/universal life):** $_____ | Cash value can be borrowed against now or used as trust collateral |
| **Current beneficiary(ies):** Who is named? | Must be updated if the trust should receive the benefit |
| **Policy owner:** Who owns the policy? | Usually Grandma, but must be confirmed |
| **Premium amount and frequency:** $___/month or year | Determines ongoing cost to keep the policy active |
| **Loans against the policy:** Any outstanding? | Reduces effective death benefit |
| **Riders:** Accidental death, long-term care, waiver of premium? | May have additional value |

### How Life Insurance Fits Into the Trust

The attorney and CPA will evaluate three options:

**Option 1: Change the beneficiary to the trust**

- Death benefit flows into the trust at Grandma's passing
- Trust uses the funds per its governing rules (reserves, debt payoff, distributions)
- Simple to execute — just a beneficiary change form
- **Downside:** Death benefit may be included in Grandma's taxable estate

**Option 2: Create an Irrevocable Life Insurance Trust (ILIT)**

- A separate trust owns the policy
- Death benefit is paid to the ILIT, then distributed per ILIT terms
- **Key advantage:** Keeps the death benefit OUT of Grandma's taxable estate entirely
- **Key requirement:** Grandma cannot be the owner of the policy — ownership must transfer to the ILIT. If Grandma dies within 3 years of the transfer, the IRS may include the benefit in her estate anyway (the "3-year rule")
- **Best for:** Large death benefits where estate tax is a concern
- More complex and has legal setup costs ($2K–$5K)

**Option 3: Keep the policy as-is (beneficiaries unchanged)**

- Death benefit goes directly to named beneficiaries outside the trust
- No estate tax planning benefit
- No trust integration
- Simplest, but may not align with overall plan

**The right choice depends on:**

- The size of Grandma's total estate relative to the federal estate tax exemption
- The death benefit amount
- Whether the policy has cash value that could be used now
- Whether the 3-year rule for ILIT transfers is a concern given Grandma's age

**The attorney and CPA will make this recommendation. Your job is to bring the policy.**

## SECTION 8: QUESTIONS TO BRING TO THE FIRST MEETING

### For the Trust Attorney

1. Can Berdia's solar lease interest be assigned to a trust? Does the lease allow it?
2. What type of trust is recommended — grantor vs. non-grantor, revocable vs. irrevocable?
3. Should the life insurance policy be held inside the trust, moved to an ILIT, or left as-is?
4. What are the estate tax implications given the total asset picture?
5. How should the restaurant transfer be structured to avoid triggering unnecessary taxes?
6. What successor trustee provisions do you recommend?
7. How do we handle the 3-year lookback rule if we transfer the life insurance policy?
8. What is the estimated cost and timeline for trust formation, LLC setup, and operating agreements?

### For the CPA

1. What is the restaurant's actual EBITDA based on the financials provided?
2. What trust structure (grantor/non-grantor) is most tax-efficient for this family?
3. How will solar income be taxed — as rental income, ordinary income, or other?
4. What are the gift tax implications of transferring assets into the trust?
5. Does Grandma's total estate exceed the federal estate tax exemption? If the exemption drops in 2026, does that change the answer?
6. How should LLC income be structured for tax efficiency — pass-through to trust or retained?
7. What bookkeeping system should the restaurant adopt going forward?
8. What quarterly/annual filings will the trust and LLCs require?

**Budget:** $3K–$8K for initial engagement (financial cleanup + tax structure advisory). Ongoing: $2K–$5K/year for trust and LLC filings.
