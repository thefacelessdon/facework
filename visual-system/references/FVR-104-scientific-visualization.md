# FVR-104 — Scientific Visualization

## Question

How can a visual representation reduce ambiguity while keeping its claim,
method, and limitations inspectable?

## Reference field

Plots, network diagrams, biological taxonomies, geological sections, anatomical
illustration, mathematical notation, uncertainty displays, and systems models.

## Observations

1. Position and length usually support more exact comparison than decorative
   area, volume, or perspective.
2. Labels, scales, units, legends, and source notes are part of the argument.
3. A useful diagram states what its nodes, edges, directions, colors, and sizes
   mean.
4. Uncertainty can be visualized rather than suppressed.
5. A compelling image can overstate confidence when its construction is hidden.
6. Different resolutions may be needed for orientation, analysis, and detail.

## Extracted principles

### Encoding is a contract

Every visible property used to carry meaning must have a stable definition in
the artifact.

### Show the limit of the claim

Omission, uncertainty, sample, projection, and inference should be disclosed at
the level needed to prevent a false reading.

### Prefer inspectability to spectacle

Visual beauty may support attention and memory, but it cannot outrank the
reader's ability to understand what is being asserted.

### Resolution is contextual

Overview and detail may use different representations while preserving the same
underlying relationships.

## Limits and cautions

- The appearance of measurement can give qualitative models false authority.
- Network diagrams often imply causation when they show only association.
- Three-dimensional and animated views may obscure exact comparison.
- Simplification can erase populations, exceptions, or uncertainty that matter.

## FVS impact

- Establishes diagram requirements in FVS-900.
- Supports semantic color and non-color redundancy in FVS-600.
- Requires text alternatives and declared encoding.
- Rejects ornamental “systems diagrams” whose relationships cannot be defined.
